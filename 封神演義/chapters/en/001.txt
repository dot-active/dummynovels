Chapter One: King Zhou Burns Incense at the Temple of Nüwa

An opening verse in the ancient style:

Since chaos first divided, Pangu came before all things;
The Supreme Ultimate split in two—Two Forms, Four Images it brings.
At the Rat Hour the heavens formed; at Ox the earth was laid;
At Tiger's hour came humankind; from beasts the Nest-Builder saved.
Suiren struck fire and ended the eating of raw fare;
Fuxi drew his eight trigrams, yin and yang laid bare.
Shennong ruled the world and tasted every herb in turn;
The Yellow Emperor set rites and music, wedlock's bonds to learn.
Shaohao and the Five Emperors saw the people's goods increase;
Yu the Great tamed the floods and brought the surging waters peace.
Four hundred years of tranquil reign came in prosperous ease,
Till Jie the tyrant-king laid all kingly virtue to disease—
He dallied day and night with Meixi, given to wine and lust;
Cheng Tang arose at Bo and swept away the reek and rust.
He exiled Jie to Nanchao, relieved the suffering of man;
Like clouds and mist dissolving, all revived and life began.

Thirty-one generations passed until the Shang line's end came near,
The Shang house's thread was severed, its pattern dark and drear:
He ravaged law and discipline, cut off all kinship's ties,
Killed wife and child on slanders from treacherous allies;
Defiled the inner palace with his love for Daji's charms,
Set scorching copper pillars and scorpion-pits for arms.
From the Deer Terrace his levies ground the people to the bone,
And sighs and bitter grievances rose up to Heaven's throne.
Those who spoke straight he burned; the righteous he cut through;
He split open pregnant women as his madness grew.
He trusted villains, cast aside all governance and rule,
Banished his teachers—his nature warped, a brutal fool.
Let rites and sacrifices lapse, let the temples fall to waste,
Gave heart and soul to perverse craft and trickery in haste.
He clung to sinners, feared nothing—drank and raged like a kite in flight,
A hawk swooping on all who lived, the tyrant of his might.

The Lord of the West bowed at Shang's court and was jailed at Youli keep;
Weizi took the altar vessels and fled into the dark and deep.
High Heaven shook in wrath and sent down calamities galore,
Like crossing a vast ocean that had no floor.
All beneath heaven withered; ten thousand people's hate poured free;
Then Ziya emerged—a true sage, a holy man was he—
He fished each day beside the stream to find a lord to aid;
A flying bear appeared in dreams—at Qishan, hunt the glade.
He rode with Zhou and helped direct the affairs of court and state;
Two-thirds of the realm already turned before they sealed Zhou's fate.
King Wen at last fulfilled his charge and then his mandate fell;
King Wu took up his father's work and labored long and well.
At Mengjin he convened eight hundred lords in mighty array,
To chastise that fierce tyrant and punish every wrong his way.
At Muye, in the grey dawn hour of the jiazi day,
The enemy's own vanguard wheeled and cut their masters' way.
As walls collapse they bowed their heads to earth in mass reverence—
Blood flowed and floated spear-shafts; fat streamed like a dense river's sense.
He had barely donned his battle-dress when the realm was set to right,
And added even greater glory to Cheng Tang's ancient light.
He released the horses at Huashan and put all warfare past,
And opened for our Zhou eight hundred years of peace to last.
The white flag flew—the tyrant-lord was dead;
The souls of fallen warriors found their rest instead.
Heaven raised a sage all hailed as Father Shang—
On the Investiture Platform, jade rosters rang:
Each great and lesser hero took a destined divine place,
And the tale of Shang and Zhou is told through all of time and space.

Cheng Tang was a descendant of the Yellow Emperor, of the clan-name Zi. In ancient times, the second consort of Emperor Ku, Jiandi, prayed at the High Matchmaker's shrine and received an omen of a dark bird, and thereupon gave birth to Qi. Qi served under Tang and Yu as Minister of Instruction and taught the people with great merit; he was enfeoffed at Shang. The line passed through thirteen generations to Tai Yi, who was known as Cheng Tang. He heard that Yi Yin was farming in the wilds of Youxin, delighting in the Way of Yao and Shun—a man of great virtue. Three times Tang dispatched an envoy with silk and jade to invite him, yet he dared not employ Yi Yin on his own authority and so presented him to the Son of Heaven. Jie the tyrant trusted slanders, drove away the worthy, and would not employ Yi Yin, who thus returned to Tang's service. Later Jie gave himself ever more to debauchery, executed the loyal minister Guan Longfeng, and the multitude dared not speak plainly. Tang sent men to mourn him. Jie raged and imprisoned Tang at Xiatai. In time Tang was released and returned to his realm. Outside the city he saw a man spreading nets on all four sides and praying: "Let those who fall from heaven, rise from the earth, or come from the four directions—all fall into my net!" Tang removed three sides of the net, leaving only one, and offered a different prayer: "Let those who wish to go left go left, those who wish to go right go right, those who wish to go high go high, those who wish to go low go low; only those who will not follow their own nature shall fall into my net!" The men south of the Han River heard of this and said, "Tang's virtue is supreme!" More than forty states came over to him. Jie's tyranny grew daily worse and the people could not survive. Yi Yin helped Tang chastise Jie and exiled Jie to Nanchao. The lords held a great assembly; Tang withdrew and took his place among them. All the lords put forward Tang as the Son of Heaven. Tang then assumed the throne, making Bo his capital. In the first year, yiwei, Tang on the throne abolished Jie's tyrannical laws, followed the people's wishes, and all near and far came to him. Because of Jie's wickedness there had been drought for seven years; Cheng Tang prayed at the Mulberry Grove and Heaven sent great rains. He also had coins cast from the gold of Zhuang Mountain to save the people's lives. He composed the music called "Da Huo"—huo meaning "protection," signifying that Tang in his generous benevolence could protect and shelter all living people. He reigned thirteen years and then died, having lived one hundred years. The dynasty endured for six hundred and forty years, through the following line down to Shang Shou, where it ended:

Cheng Tang — Tai Jia — Wo Ding — Tai Geng — Xiao Jia — Yong Ji
Tai Wu — Zhong Ding — Wai Ren — He Dan Jia — Zu Yi — Zu Xin
Wo Jia — Zu Ding — Nan Geng — Yang Jia — Pan Geng — Xiao Xin
Xiao Yi — Wu Ding — Zu Geng — Zu Jia — Lin Xin — Geng Ding
Wu Yi — Tai Ding — Di Yi — King Zhou

King Zhou was the third son of Di Yi. Di Yi had three sons: the eldest was Weizi Qi; the second was Weizi Yan; the third was Prince Shou. While Di Yi was strolling in the imperial garden with his assembled civil and military officers admiring the peonies, a beam collapsed in the Feiyun Pavilion, and Prince Shou held up the beam and replaced the pillar with his bare hands—his strength was matchless. Because Prime Minister Shang Rong, Senior Grand Master Mei Bo, Zhao Qi, and others submitted memorials to establish an Eastern Palace heir, Prince Shou was made Crown Prince. Later Di Yi reigned thirty years and died, entrusting the young successor to Grand Preceptor Wen Zhong, and Prince Shou was duly installed as Son of Heaven under the name King Zhou, with Zhaoge as his capital. In civil affairs he had Grand Preceptor Wen Zhong; in military affairs he had the Pacifier of the Realm, Martial King Huang Feihu; the civil sufficed to pacify the land, the martial sufficed to settle the kingdom. In the Central Palace his principal consort was Queen Jiang; in the West Palace was Lady Huang; in the Qingqing Palace was Lady Yang. All three palace consorts were of chaste and tranquil character, gentle and virtuous. King Zhou sat at ease in an age of peace: ten thousand people worked contentedly, the winds and rains came in their seasons, the realm was at peace and the people prosperous; the four barbarian peoples folded their hands in submission, the eight directions paid homage, and all eight hundred vassal lords came to court at Shang. Four great lords each commanded two hundred lesser lords: the Eastern Marquis Jiang Huanchu, residing in Donglu; the Southern Marquis E Chongyu; the Western Marquis Ji Chang; and the Northern Marquis Chong Houhu. Each marquis commanded two hundred lesser lords—eight hundred in total under Shang.

In the seventh year of King Zhou's reign, in the second month of spring, news suddenly arrived at Zhaoge that the seventy-two clans of lords of the Northern Sea, under Yuan Futong and others, had rebelled. Grand Preceptor Wen Zhong received the imperial edict and set out to pacify the north. No more of that for now.

One day King Zhou held early court and assembled his civil and military officers. Behold:

Auspicious vapors drifted in soft billows; upon the golden throne-hall sat the king on high.
Propitious light coiled and wound; before the white jade steps ranked all the lords nearby.
Eight hundred gold censers breathed fragrant dark sandalwood;
The pearl curtains were raised on high.
Musk and orchid fragrance wreathed the precious fans;
The pheasant-tail screens swayed gently to and fro.

The Son of Heaven asked his attending officers: "Let those with memorials come forward; if there is no business, the court is dismissed." Before the words were done, one man stepped out from the right-hand rank, prostrated himself at the golden steps, raised his ivory tablet high above his head, and cried out his reverences: "Your servant Shang Rong, guilty of holding the Prime Ministership and governing the court's affairs, has something to report. Tomorrow is the fifteenth day of the third month, the holy birthday of the Lady Nüwa. I respectfully request that Your Majesty's palanquin proceed to the Temple of Nüwa to offer incense."

The king said: "What merit or virtue does Nüwa possess to warrant Our going in Our imperial palanquin to offer incense?"

Shang Rong memorialized: "The Lady Nüwa is a divine woman of high antiquity, born with sacred virtue. In that time, Gonggong struck his head against Mount Buzhou; the sky tilted to the northwest and the earth sank in the southeast. Nüwa gathered five-colored stones and smelted them to mend the blue sky, thus rendering merit to the people of the land. The common people established solemn worship in gratitude. Now Zhaoge venerates this auspicious spirit, so that the four seasons may be propitious, the dynastic fortune may endure long, winds and rains come in their season, and calamities be quietly averted. She is a righteous deity who blesses the realm and shelters the people; Your Majesty ought to go and offer incense."

The king said: "We approve your memorial." King Zhou returned to the inner palace. The edict was proclaimed; the next day the Son of Heaven mounted his palanquin, with both ranks of civil and military officers in attendance, and proceeded to the Temple of Nüwa to offer incense. This visit of King Zhou's was better never made: because of this incense-burning, the four seas fell into chaos and the people lost their livelihoods. As the old saying goes: "Spread your hook and line across the river, and from this day you will fish up trouble." How so? The following verse confirms it:

The Son of Heaven's imperial procession left the phoenix city;
Flags and banners gleamed auspiciously against jade hairpins and silken sashes.
Dragon-light swords gave off the color of wind and cloud;
Red feather standards swayed, heavy with the essence of sun and moon.
Dyke willows at dawn shared the dew from the immortal's palm;
Stream flowers shone brightly on the pure jade-green robes.
Wishing to know the grandeur of the royal progress? Behold:
Ten thousand states, clothed and crowned, bowing before the sage and holy king.

The procession left through the south gate of Zhaoge; every household burned incense and lit lanterns, every doorway was hung with colored streamers and spread with mats. Three thousand iron cavalry, eight hundred of the imperial guard—Martial King Huang Feihu protecting the palanquin, the full court of civil and military officers in train. They arrived at the Temple of Nüwa. The Son of Heaven descended from his palanquin, ascended to the great hall, and burned incense in the censer; all civil and military officers followed their ranks, bowed in reverence, and paid their respects. King Zhou looked about at the magnificent decorations of the hall. Behold:

Before the hall, all was glorious, adorned in five colors and gold;
Golden lads in pairs bore banner-staffs and streaming pennants bright;
Jade maids in pairs offered as-you-will scepters.
Jade hooks hung slantwise; half a new moon hung suspended in the void;
Precious curtains billowed gently; ten thousand pairs of five-colored phoenixes faced the Dipper.
Beside the canopy of emerald silk, all was dancing cranes and soaring luan-birds;
On the dark-incense precious throne, carved walking dragons and flying phoenixes.
These rare and wondrous colors surpassed all ordinary things;
Golden censers breathed out auspicious mist;
Thread upon thread of happy omens sent purple vapors rising,
And silver candles blazed with brilliant light.
The king was gazing at the decorations of the travel-palace when a wild gust of wind chilled him through the marrow.

King Zhou was admiring the orderly arrangement of the palace halls and the richness of the towers and pavilions when suddenly a great gust of wind rolled up the curtains and exposed the sacred image of Nüwa. Her face was of surpassing beauty, radiant with auspicious light, a countenance of heaven and beauty of the world, as lifelike as if she breathed. Truly she was like a fairy of the blossom-palace descending to the mortal world, or the Moon Lady coming down from her palace in the sky. The old saying goes: "When a state is about to flourish, there will be good omens; when a state is about to perish, there will be ill ones." King Zhou, at one glance, felt his soul wander and his heart fill with desire. He said to himself: We are the most honored as Son of Heaven and possess the six inner apartments and three palaces, yet none within them holds such beauty. The king said: "Bring the four treasures of the study." The attending officers quickly brought them. The Son of Heaven dipped the purple brush deep and wrote a poem on the whitewashed wall of the travel-palace:

"Phoenix and simurgh precious curtains frame a scene beyond compare,
All artfully adorned with gilded gold in intricate array.
Winding distant mountains fly a shade of jade-green air;
Fluttering dancing sleeves are mirrored in rosy-cloud display.
Pear blossoms in the rain compete in tender, lovely grace;
Peonies veiled in mist display their seductive, alluring face.
If only this bewitching beauty could stir and come alive,
I would take her back to the Changle Palace, there to serve her lord and thrive."

When the Son of Heaven had finished composing, Prime Minister Shang Rong came forward to memorialize: "Nüwa is a righteous deity of the highest antiquity and the presiding spirit of Zhaoge's fortune. Your old servant invited Your Majesty's palanquin here to offer incense and pray for blessings, so that the ten thousand people might work in contentment, the rains and winds come in season, and the fires of war be quieted. Now Your Majesty has composed a poem that dishonors and profanes the sacred illustrious one without any reverence or sincerity, thus incurring guilt before the gods and spirits. This is not the rite proper to a Son of Heaven offering prayers. I humbly beg Your Majesty to wash it away with water. I fear that should the people of the realm see it, they would spread word that the sage ruler lacks virtuous governance." The king said: "We saw Nüwa's countenance of transcendent beauty, unmatched in the world, and so composed a poem in praise—there is no other meaning. Let Our minister speak no more of it. Moreover, We are the most honored under heaven; leaving it for the ten thousand people to see will show them that the Lady's beauty is matchless, and will also preserve Our own brushwork." Having spoken, he returned to court. The civil and military officers silently shook their heads; none dared say anything; all clenched their lips and withdrew. A verse to confirm it:

The phoenix palanquin and dragon carriage left the imperial city;
They came to burn incense and pray before the heroine of women.
They knew only to pray for the people's happiness and prosperity—
Who could have guessed that one poem would fill ten thousand with dread?
Before long, a vixen would be enthroned as Empress Mother;
Before their eyes, jackals and wolves would don the court's regalia.
High Heaven shows its signs, all being thus arranged—
And only caused the heroes of the age to sigh in helpless grief.

The Son of Heaven returned to the throne and ascended to the Hall of Longde. The officers came forward in greeting and then departed. It being the time of the Full Moon, the consorts of the three palaces came to pay their respects to the king: Queen Jiang of the Central Palace, Lady Huang of the West Palace, and Lady Yang of the Qingqing Palace; they paid their court and withdrew. We set this matter aside for now.

Now let it be told that on the Lady Nüwa's birthday, the fifteenth of the third month, she had gone to the Palace of Fire Clouds to pay her respects to the Three Sages—Fuxi, the Flame Emperor, and the Yellow Emperor—and was returning. Descending from her azure simurgh, she took her seat upon the precious throne. After the jade maids and golden lads completed their obeisance, the Lady suddenly raised her eyes and saw the verses written on the whitewashed wall. She was greatly angered and cursed: "Yin Shou, you immoral and benighted king! You do not think to cultivate your conduct and establish virtue to preserve the realm; instead you dare to defy Heaven and write verses profaning me. How utterly detestable! I consider that Cheng Tang defeated Jie and ruled the realm; the dynastic destiny of six hundred and more years is now exhausted. If I give him no recompense, my divine power will count for nothing." She thereupon called for her Azure Mist page-boy to saddle the azure simurgh and fly once to Zhaoge. No more of that.

Now it is told that the two princes Yin Jiao and Yin Hong came to pay their respects to their royal father—this Yin Jiao would later appear on the Investiture Scroll as the "Tai Sui of the Year," and Yin Hong as the "God of the Five Grains": both would become famous divine generals. Just as they were performing their prostrations, two beams of red light shot from their heads straight into the sky. The Lady, traveling on her way, was blocked in her cloud-path by this energy; she looked down, saw that King Zhou still had twenty-eight years of dynastic fortune remaining, and judged that she must not act rashly. She returned temporarily to her travel-palace, displeased at heart. She called the Rosy Cloud page-boy to bring from the rear palace the golden gourd and placed it at the foot of the cinnabar steps; she lifted the gourd's lid and pointed with one hand. From the gourd there issued a white light, as thin as a thread, more than four or five zhang in height. Above this white light there floated a banner, its radiance dividing into five colors and shining with a thousand auspicious rays; it was called the "Banner for Summoning Demons." In a short while a sorrowful wind rose, a gloomy mist spread far and wide, dark clouds gathered on all four sides, and after several gusts of wind, all the demons throughout the world arrived at the travel-palace to await the Lady's command. The Lady told Rosy Cloud: "Have all the demons in other places withdraw; keep only the three demons from Xuanyuan Mound to wait upon me." The three demons entered the palace and bowed in greeting, saying: "May the Lady's holy life endure without end!" The three demons were: a thousand-year fox spirit, a nine-headed pheasant spirit, and a jade pipa spirit. They prostrated themselves at the foot of the cinnabar steps. The Lady said: "You three demons heed Our secret edict: the vital energy of Cheng Tang has grown dim and the realm is about to be lost; the phoenix cries at Mount Qi—the holy ruler of Western Zhou has already appeared. Heaven's will is fixed; destiny has so determined. You three demons are to conceal your demonic natures, take on forms within the palace precincts, confuse and disorder the king's heart; and when King Wu launches his campaign against Zhou, assist him in completing the work—but you must not harm the multitudes of living people. When the task is complete, you shall each attain the reward of righteous immortality." Having given her instructions, the three demons kowtowed in gratitude, transformed into a clear wind, and departed. Truly:

The fox spirits heard the divine decree and worked their demon art—
To bring down Cheng Tang's six-hundred-year dynastic fortune.

A verse to confirm it:

In the middle of the third month, the palanquin went to burn incense;
One poem sent calamity flying in.
He knew only to wield his brush and display his cleverness—
He did not know that on this day his dynasty was lost.

We set aside the Lady Nüwa's charge to the three demons for now.

Now it is told that King Zhou, after his visit to burn incense, had seen Nüwa's beauty and thought of her morning and night, forgetting both heat and cold, neglecting food and sleep. Every time he looked upon his six apartments and three palaces, it was as though he gazed at dust-cakes and mud-broth—not worth a second glance. All the day long he could not put the matter from his mind and was moody and dispirited. One day he ascended to the Xianqing Hall in his palanquin, with his constant attendants at his side. King Zhou suddenly collected himself and commanded his officers to summon the Grand Counselor Fei Zhong.—This Fei Zhong was a favorite of King Zhou's. Since Grand Preceptor Wen Zhong had received the imperial edict and led the great army on a distant campaign to the Northern Sea, the king had come to favor Fei Zhong and You Hun above all others. These two daily confused the king's clear judgment with slanders and flattering words, and King Zhou never failed to follow them. It is ever thus: when the realm is about to fall, treacherous ministers hold the power.—Shortly Fei Zhong came before him. The king said: "When We went to burn incense at Nüwa's temple, We happened to see her face—radiant and beautiful, without equal in the world. None in Our three palaces and six apartments matches Our desire. What is to be done? Have you any plan to ease Our heart?" Fei Zhong memorialized: "Your Majesty is the most honored under heaven, possessing the four seas. In virtue You match Yao and Shun; all that is under heaven belongs to Your Majesty. What thought cannot be made real? What difficulty lies here? Your Majesty need only issue an edict tomorrow, to be proclaimed through the lords of the four quarters: let each lord select one hundred beautiful women to fill Your Majesty's court. Why should one fear that the matchless beauties of the world would not enter Your Majesty's selection?" King Zhou was greatly pleased: "Your memorial accords most perfectly with Our mind. Tomorrow at early court We shall issue the edict. You may withdraw for now." Thereupon he commanded his palanquin and returned to the inner palace. As for what came after—listen to the next chapter to learn.
