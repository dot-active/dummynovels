Chapter One: Porcelain Town

At the busy wharf, where people came and went and cargo was unloaded and loaded onto boats, a young gentleman of seventeen or eighteen, dressed in a blue-gray daopao robe of patterned gauze with ruyi cloud motifs, stepped off a boat with an attendant of about twenty. Arriving in bustling Jingdezhen, his eyes were full of curiosity and delight. This town, which held the economic lifeblood of all Raozhou in its grip, was truly something that had to be seen to be believed.

Shops lined the Changjiang River row upon row. Besides the stores selling blue-and-white porcelain, there were inns, taverns, teahouses and cloth shops, and near the wharf were warehouses large and small, while far off the curling smoke of kiln fires rose from the workshops. It was enough to leave him dazzled, with more to look at than his eyes could take in.

He had come all this way on purpose, so how could he merely skim past? The most important thing, then, was to find a place to stay, so that he could linger a few days and explore this great town properly.

Jingdezhen was, of course, an ancient town with a long history of porcelain-making. It took the name "Jingde" during the Jingde reign of the Northern Song; by the Yuan dynasty its blue-and-white porcelain had matured and its fame had spread far and wide; and after the Ming took power, the court set up an imperial manufactory on Zhushan to fire porcelain for the throne, so that official and private kilns flourished side by side. After several hundred years it had long since become the place where the porcelain of the whole realm came together.

Zhu Changqing slowly took in the bustling scene of people and horses jostling along the Changjiang River. As Heir Apparent of the Prince of Huai, it was only right that he should pay attention to the lives of the common people in his father's fiefdom. And since he was also in low spirits and had taken a trip to clear his mind as his excuse, he had decided to visit Jingdezhen and catch a glimpse of this far-famed legend. Yet he had no wish to disturb the people, and wanted even more to see how ordinary folk truly lived, so he had chosen to travel incognito.

"Would the young master like to take a room first, and observe the people's lives once we're settled?" Li Zong, his guard, spotted an inn ahead and asked Zhu Changqing in a low voice.

"Very well." Zhu Changqing nodded, then strode toward the Yuelai Inn up ahead.

The Yuelai Inn looked fresh and elegant, and its business was thriving. People were constantly going in and out, and there were several waiters greeting guests inside. Li Zong went to the counter and asked a waiter for a superior guest room, and followed him upstairs.

Pushing open the door, they found the room tidy enough. A wooden bed stood against the inner wall, its bedding neatly laid out; by the window was a couch, and beside it a square table with a few wooden stools, the table set with a teapot and teacups. The furnishings could hardly be called luxurious, but they were refined and comfortable. Li Zong told the waiter to bring a pot of hot tea, and only after the man had gone did he say with a worried look, "Though it's a superior room, it's still rather humble. I'm afraid the young master will have to put up with it."

"I'm not putting up with anything. If the common people can stay here, so can I." Zhu Changqing's expression was gentle, his tone easy.

"The young master is right. It was thoughtless of me." Li Zong poured hot tea into a teacup and offered it with both hands.

"I know you feel for me." Zhu Changqing took the tea and gestured for Li Zong to drink as well, and only then did Li Zong pour himself a cup.

"Even though everyone who deserved punishment in the dispute over the succession back then has been punished, His Highness still takes no interest in the Princess Consort or the Heir. I fear the injustice the Heir has suffered is unmatched among all the imperial princes of the Great Ming." Li Zong sipped his tea and spoke quietly on Zhu Changqing's behalf, all his heartache written on his face.

"Don't speak carelessly of Father Prince," Zhu Changqing said to stop him.

"I won't speak of it... but does the wrong done to the Princess Consort and the Heir cease to exist just because I don't? The ones who caused the trouble have been dead for several years now, yet His Highness is still cold toward the Heir."

"The elder brother and the concubine Father loved most are both gone, so naturally he lays his resentment at the feet of me and Mother Consort. But 'there are no parents in the world who are in the wrong.' A son can only follow his father's wishes. He cannot go about complaining all day because he's fallen out of favor."

"Not complaining, just feeling low, and coming out to ease the weight in his chest," Li Zong "hit back" at his gentle and dutiful Heir.

Zhu Changqing gave a wry smile. "I can only tell you when I'm feeling low, and you actually use it to 'stab' me?"

"Because the Heir is so good, so good that he bears every slight without resentment. It's only that I ache inside, and I have to voice my grievance somehow." As he spoke, Li Zong's eyes seemed to cloud over faintly.

"If resentment could solve our difficulties, then resentment would mean something. Since it can't, why bother?" Zhu Changqing finished the hot tea in his cup and simply poured himself another. Li Zong meant to rush forward and do it for him, but Zhu Changqing gave him a look that said there was no need.

"Even though I am the Heir, there's no need to trouble others over everything I can do with my own hands. See? Isn't this faster, getting another sip of hot tea?"

"Young master..." Li Zong steadied his feelings. He understood Zhu Changqing far too well. Because of the Prince's favoritism, he had even toyed with the idea of replacing his heir, and had confined the Heir, then only thirteen, in a secret chamber. If the affair hadn't come to light, and the elder half-brother and his foster mother Wang Aier hadn't both been put to death according to the law, perhaps there would be no Heir today at all? Yet his Heir was still filial and kind, with not a trace of bitterness. He was truly a rare scion of the imperial house.

But he simply couldn't help feeling for his Heir...

"Whether as Heir or as Prince of Huai, what one should strive for is responsibility, not position. What makes a prince noble is not his rank but his duty to the common people. For as long as one holds this seat, one must fulfill one's duty for that long, and only then can one be worthy of the imperial clan."

This was his Heir, all over! Li Zong did not know what to say. Having endured all manner of hardship at so young an age, he was still bright and open of heart, radiant and selfless. Li Zong could not understand why such a legitimate eldest son could not win his father's affection, and how unjust Heaven was.

Note: In the Ming dynasty the daopao ("Taoist robe") was not clothing worn only by Taoist priests; it was also a common form of everyday dress for scholars.
