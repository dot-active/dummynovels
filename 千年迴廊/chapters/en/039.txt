Chapter Two: First Meeting

After tea, the baggage was put roughly in order, and Zhu Changqing took Li Zong to continue along the bank of the Changjiang River.

They had gone only a short way when they heard a commotion from far off. A young gentleman in an indigo yisa robe of patterned silk and a square scholar's cap was touching the blue-and-white porcelain in one of the shops, picking pieces up now and then to toy with them, when a girl of sixteen or seventeen struck his hand with a thin bamboo switch and said angrily, "Every one of these vases, jars, cups and plates, big or small, you must treat with respect. Your attitude is so careless that you have no right to touch them."

The girl wore a peach-pink blouse of plain silk over a deep purple horse-face skirt, her black hair pinned up with only a single silver hairpin. Young as she was, she had a fearless air about her, and she scolded with a force that fairly whipped up a wind.

The young gentleman, stung, shook his hand and said, red in the face, "You shrew! They're only a few porcelains. My family has no end of them. I only looked. Does it cost you a few pounds of flesh? And you actually hit me?"

"Stop shaking your hand. If you shake it into a bowl or plate and break it, you'll have to pay," the girl warned him with blazing eyes, and went on, "Your family is your family. Why should I care how much blue-and-white you have at home? Here is here. You should know how many people it takes to finish each of these pieces. More people than can be counted across the whole Great Ming love it, and every day they gather from every direction for the sake of this very porcelain. And you, young master, are so frivolous, defiling it here."

"You... even if I broke a whole shop's worth of porcelain I could afford to pay for it! The way you look down on people, who are you trying to scare?"

"Go ahead and try," the girl snorted. "If you really smash a whole shop of porcelain, I'll report you to the officials and let the magistrate decide the matter! What I want is not just to be paid back the price."

"Then... what else can you do to me?"

"Throwing your weight around and bullying the market is called picking a quarrel and wrecking a shop. It won't be settled by paying a few taels of silver."

By now the onlookers had crowded all around, buzzing with talk. Some said the girl was the shop owner's daughter, surnamed Wu, named Jingyu; others said the young gentleman was the legitimate son of a wealthy merchant surnamed Zhang from Fuliang county town, a notorious idler and fop, who had somehow wandered into Jingdezhen, and so the two had started shouting at each other in the porcelain shop.

Wu Jingyu did not give an inch. She called out all the shop's assistants and had them stand in a row behind her like a "wall of people," ready to give the fop a beating and drag him to the authorities at the slightest overstep. Zhang Huan had been about to bluster another couple of lines, something like "I'm not one to be frightened," to bolster his courage, when his eye happened to fall upon a face in the crowd that looked vaguely familiar.

"Y-Your Highness the Heir?" He suddenly wilted, and began to stammer.

"The Heir?" Following Zhang Huan's gaze, the crowd turned as one to look at Zhu Changqing, and even made way for him unprompted. Zhu Changqing looked a little awkward. He had never meant to disturb anyone, which was why he'd chosen to travel incognito, and now he had been recognized in an instant. It was truly beyond his expectations.

Some in the crowd still didn't understand what was going on, and asked timidly in a low voice, "Which Heir?"

"Here, of course, it's the Heir of the Prince of Huai. Whose Heir could it be?" The answer was nothing short of a blow to the head.

Zhu Changqing had no choice. He gave a quiet sigh inwardly, and stepped forward along the path that had been cleared for him. The people dropped to their knees one after another, bowing their heads and crying, "We pay our respects to the Heir."

"Please rise, everyone," Zhu Changqing said gently. Then he turned to Zhang Huan. "Young Master Zhang certainly has leisure and refined tastes, to come all this way to Jingdezhen to cause trouble."

"I just had nothing to do, and came to Jingdezhen to stroll about..." Zhang Huan went on stammering his reply. "I didn't know the Heir also had the leisure to stroll about Jingdezhen... I offended the Heir... Zhang Huan is at fault..." He felt as though his tongue and teeth were fighting each other, and he was nearly incoherent.

Now it was Wu Jingyu's turn to be stunned. It was clearly her family's porcelain shop that this Zhang Huan, the fop, had harassed, yet it was as if the Heir were the injured party, and he was actually apologizing to the Heir? She was still turning this over in her mind when she heard the Heir say, "Why do you apologize to me? This porcelain shop isn't mine."

"Then..." Zhang Huan was still hemming and hawing.

"You should apologize to Miss Wu."

Zhang Huan was unwilling. He hadn't wrecked the shop or broken a single bowl, and he'd even taken a blow. How could he be willing to apologize? Seeing this, Zhu Changqing patted Zhang Huan on the shoulder. "Do you understand the meaning of 'what one person uses is made ready by the work of a hundred artisans'?"

"I've read it... isn't that what Mencius said?" Zhang Huan's face flushed again, his voice so small it was almost inaudible.

"A single piece of blue-and-white takes the toil of who knows how many people to make. It is shipped across the seas to Japan, the Southern Seas, and the lands of the West, and countless people long for it and cannot get it. And you, with a few coins in your purse, dare to treat it with contempt. Have you read all your books for nothing?"

For Zhang Huan, reading had in truth been only a formality, something to get by on with his parents and his tutor, and he had never seriously used the principles in books to discipline himself. Even so, goaded by Zhu Changqing like this, he had to think of his own face, and he at least understood that in front of the Heir he could only act the grandson and not the lord. So, with a long face, he made a low apology to Wu Jingyu.

"Miss Wu! I'm sorry..." Under Zhu Changqing's "supervision," Zhang Huan finally bowed his head and admitted his fault.

Note: The yisa was one of the common robes worn by men in the Ming dynasty, with pleats below the waist, and in the middle and late Ming it was also worn for everyday and social occasions.
