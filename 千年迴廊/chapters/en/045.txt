Chapter Eight: A Retreating Figure

Every evening, Wu Jingyu would come to the garden residence to pay her respects to Zhu Changqing, and also to keep a close eye on the dishes the kitchen prepared. Today the Heir had come back a little late, and "loaded with spoils," and Wu Jingyu couldn't help laughing.

"Has the Heir been plundering homes and robbing houses in Jingdezhen?"

But at Wu Jingyu's teasing, Zhu Changqing's face turned red again, and he muttered, "I was recognized at the tavern, and it ended up like this. The people of this town are too warm-hearted. They almost wanted to send their daughters to the palace to be the Heir's consort... they even asked whether I was betrothed."

Wu Jingyu burst into laughter, and then said, "The Heir isn't just plundering homes, he's even been 'abducting maidens.'"

"Please don't talk nonsense..." Zhu Changqing was flustered, and his face grew redder still.

Wu Jingyu did her best to stop laughing, and urged Zhi'er, "Why don't you hurry and bring the 'tribute' from the four corners into the house for the Heir?"

Watching his Heir in that awkward state, with his weak spot completely pinned by Miss Wu, Li Zong followed Zhi'er into the house, shaking his head with a laugh. Seeing his Heir so embarrassed was strangely amusing.

"I think we can skip supper tonight... With so much food, I've already eaten all afternoon, and I'd have to keep eating." A sudden idea struck Zhu Changqing, and he turned to Wu Jingyu. "Why don't you have some too, Jingyu? And take some back for Master Wu and Madam Wu to enjoy."

"This is 'tribute' from the four corners of the realm! This commoner dares not."

"Consider it me, the Heir, begging you. Would that do?" Unable to win against Wu Jingyu, Zhu Changqing had no choice but to play the pitiful card.

"Then... so granted." Wu Jingyu's silvery laughter brushed past Zhu Changqing's ear, and it sounded to him like heavenly music. Zhu Changqing then told Li Zong to hand a good part of the food to Zhi'er, and the two sides finally "reached a consensus."

"You'd better keep your mouth shut tight. Don't tell anyone that the Heir bestowed this 'tribute.' If the townspeople chase me and beat me, it'll be your fault," Wu Jingyu said, looking at Zhi'er, her hands full of food, and not forgetting to tease her, and while she was at it she struck at Zhu Changqing too, mocking him alongside.

Zhi'er held back her laughter and played along with Wu Jingyu.

"My dear miss, you're always pinning the blame on Zhi'er. Add up all these charges, and the faults are higher than Mount Lu."

"Would you have me blame the Heir?" Wu Jingyu shot Zhi'er a sidelong glance and went on, "Why don't you quickly thank him for his grace and own up to your fault?"

Hearing this, Zhi'er could only thank Zhu Changqing as if "delighted at what she saw," though with her hands full of food, her curtsy was far from proper.

"I... I..." Teased by the two girls, Zhu Changqing was at a loss for words. He wanted to argue that he wasn't that foolish, but fumbled for a long time without speaking, only his cheeks growing hotter and hotter, which embarrassed him greatly.

After a while of laughing and joking, Jingyu bowed to Zhu Changqing and took her leave. The two girls walked side by side, and Wu Jingyu even reached out and took a piece of osmanthus cake from Zhi'er to eat. Zhi'er laughed that her young lady was too greedy, eating as she walked, with no regard even for her dignity. The two chatted and laughed all the way, and Zhu Changqing watched the retreating figures, his gaze never shifting for a moment. In the glow of the setting sun, the faint gold threads on Jingyu's cloak flickered in and out of sight with her steps, and Zhu Changqing watched the occasional gleams of gold recede, as that lively, radiant retreating figure captured his gaze and captured his heart too. This was an air the princely palace had never had...

Notes:
1. Jingdezhen and Mount Lu are both in Jiangxi Province.
2. The pifeng ("cloak") was one of the common outer garments of the Ming dynasty, worn by both men and women, mostly with a front opening and a straight collar, worn over the robe; it is not what people today call a cape.
