Cultural Notes

1. Shu Brocade:
Shu brocade is a jacquard-woven silk textile produced around the Chengdu region, with a history spanning more than two thousand years — it emerged during the Qin and Han dynasties and reached its height during the Tang and Song. Made primarily from mulberry silk, its threads are first dyed in a range of colors, then interwoven warp and weft using jacquard techniques to form patterns directly during the weaving process, rather than embroidered onto the finished cloth afterward. Shu brocade is renowned for its vivid colors and finely detailed weave, and stands as one of China's traditional celebrated brocades.

The Tang dynasty marked the height of Shu brocade's development. Under Emperor Taizong of Tang, Dou Shilun, who had once served in Chengdu, created new brocade patterns that later generations came to call the "Duke of Lingyang" designs. These patterns absorbed elements of foreign cultures, centering on floral medallions and roundel motifs, bordered with pearl-roundel patterns, and further adorned with paired birds, paired beasts, and other plant and animal motifs — becoming some of the most representative patterns of Tang-dynasty Shu brocade. Shu brocade also traveled beyond the Shu region alongside merchants and traders, becoming an important commodity and vehicle of cultural exchange along the Silk Road.

In 2006, the craftsmanship of Shu brocade weaving was included in the first batch of China's National List of Intangible Cultural Heritage; in 2009, as an important component of "Traditional Chinese Sericulture and Silk Weaving Techniques," it was further inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

2. Yizhou:
Yizhou was the name of an administrative region in ancient China, its boundaries and administrative structure shifting repeatedly across the dynasties, with Chengdu serving as its principal seat for a long stretch of that history. During the Tang dynasty, the area around Chengdu was known as Yizhou, before being renamed Shu Commandery in the first year of the Tianbao era (742), and then Chengdu Prefecture in the second year of the Zhide era (757). As a historical name for the Shu region, "Yizhou" has long persisted in classical texts and later usage alike.

During the Three Kingdoms period, Zhuge Liang, in his Longzhong Plan, once said: "Yizhou is a land of natural strongholds, with fertile plains stretching a thousand li — a realm blessed by Heaven." (See the Records of the Three Kingdoms, Book of Shu, Biography of Zhuge Liang.) In the novel, when Du Cheng teasingly remarks that Su He is "so clever it's a real waste, keeping her weaving brocade in Yizhou instead of serving as some general's strategist," he is drawing precisely on this old name for the Shu region, so richly steeped in historical color.

3. Jinguan City:
Jinguan City, or "Brocade Official City," is an alternate name for Chengdu. The region's brocade-weaving tradition runs deep; during the Three Kingdoms period, the state of Shu Han established a "Brocade Commissioner" office in Chengdu, through which the government oversaw the production and sale of Shu brocade — and it was from this that Chengdu came to be called "Jinguan City." The brocade industry flourished to such an extent that it became not only a vital economic pillar of Chengdu, but also bound the very idea of "brocade" inseparably to the city itself.

When the Tang-dynasty poet Du Fu was living in Chengdu, he wrote in his poem "Happy Rain on a Spring Night": "By dawn, look where the red is damp — flowers hang heavy over Jinguan City." From then on, "Jinguan City" became an especially familiar and elegant name for Chengdu among later generations.

4. The Sogdians and the Silk Road:
The Sogdians were an ancient Central Asian people, their homeland located roughly in what is now Uzbekistan and Tajikistan, renowned for their skill in commerce. Sogdian merchants were active along the Silk Road for centuries, traveling between Central Asia and China, trading in spices, jewelry, silk textiles, and more, and playing a vital role in the cultural exchange between East and West.

During the Tang dynasty, considerable numbers of Sogdians traveled the Silk Road into China and settled in places such as Chang'an. In the novel, the Kangnawu family represents just such a Sogdian merchant family settled in Chang'an; Anmoruo, as a sabao, led his caravan back and forth along the Silk Road, giving Shu brocade the opportunity to travel, through these very merchants, to even more distant lands.

5. Xingjuan and Wenjuan:
Before the jinshi examinations of the Tang dynasty, scholars would often compile their poetry and prose into scrolls and present them to high officials or prominent literary figures, hoping to win recognition and recommendation — a practice known as "xingjuan," or "circulating scrolls." If, after an initial submission, the scholar reorganized and selected further works to present again, so as to deepen the recipient's impression of him, this second submission was called "wenjuan," or "warming the scroll." This custom was closely tied to the Tang examination system's emphasis on a scholar's existing reputation and literary talent in daily life. In the novel, Bai Jue's repeated visits to the Jingzhao Prefect's mansion to "warm his scroll" in preparation for the jinshi examination draw directly on this historical background.
