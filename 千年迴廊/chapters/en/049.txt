Chapter Twelve: Moving South

As time passed, Zhu Changqing's recuperation at the Wu garden residence brought color and spirit back to him. Yet the Qing army kept closing in, and Jingdezhen's calm would not last. Wu Jingyu had long since learned from merchants from Fuzhou that the Hongguang court in Nanjing had been destroyed, Jiangxi was in great disorder, and the Qing advance continued; merchants from Fujian and Guangdong had stopped coming to buy goods in Jingdezhen half a month earlier, and urged Head Wu to plan early and withdraw from Jingdezhen while the southern escape routes were not yet cut. With her familiarity with blue-and-white porcelain, Fuzhou had plenty of ways to carry on a livelihood, and if needed, she could move the whole family to Fuzhou and ask that merchant to help introduce her to porcelain-appraisal work.

Naturally she also knew that Zhu Changqing's staying in Jingdezhen was no long-term plan. The Longwu Emperor had taken the throne in Fuzhou, and Zhu Changqing too thought going south to Fuzhou to join the Longwu Emperor was the best course. So Jingyu set about settling the Wu family's affairs. With so many people under her, houses, fields, shops, workshops... dismissing staff and selling property could not be done in a day. The two households would go south together, looking after each other on the road, so Zhu Changqing waited until Wu Jingyu had put everything in order to set out for Fuzhou together.

During this time, Wu Jingyu had plain clothes without patterns made up for Zhu Changqing and everyone, for with rebellious mobs rising, flashy attire easily drew attention, and the prince's everyday robe in particular was starched and laundered and pressed to the bottom of a pack, never to be shown lightly. Wu Jingyu also sewed hidden pockets into the lining of Zhu Changqing's clothes and firmly stitched in several pieces of gold, to guard against petty thieves and bandits and as a reserve for emergencies. She did the same inside the clothes of herself, her two sons and Zhi'er, and treated the head accountant and the steward, who had followed Jingyu and her father for many years, in the same way.

In addition, for the convenience of daily expenses on the road, Wu Jingyu gave Li Zong and the Wu family's head accountant each a small hidden pouch holding broken silver, copper coins and the like, tied at the waist for ready use.

With everything arranged, before departure Wu Jingyu led Zhu Changqing on a special visit to Jiang's pastry shop, partly to take leave of Boss Jiang, and partly because both Zhu Changqing and she loved osmanthus cake, so they bought more to eat on the road. Boss Jiang saw them off with tears streaming down his old face, fearing that after this parting they would never meet again, and could only hope that the Prince of Huai and Wu Jingyu's whole family would reach Fuzhou safely. Before leaving, Zhu Changqing also urged Boss Jiang to move south to Fujian quickly, for the Qing iron cavalry might enter Jingdezhen at any time, and then amid the chaos of war the prospects would be grave.

Boss Jiang nodded and said hoarsely, "Our family has taken root here for generations. If we go to Fujian, leaving our homeland, in a strange place among strangers, where would we end up?"

Hearing this, Wu Jingyu asked Boss Jiang to bring paper and brush, and wrote down an address and a name, telling him that if he found this Fuzhou merchant he would naturally find her. His cakes could sell in Fuzhou just the same; both the Prince and she loved them, and she would certainly help him draw customers.

This is what they call "Heaven never seals off every road." But one must decide at once, and have the courage to take that step.

Before danger arrives, the calm before the storm most easily leads people to misjudge the situation and grow lax, thinking peaceful days can last forever. They do not know that once the storm strikes, human life is fragile, and no amount of money can necessarily save a life.

Having taken leave of Boss Jiang, Zhu Changqing and Wu Jingyu's party finally boarded the carriages, first by land, then by water. Jingyu lifted the curtain and watched Jingdezhen recede, silently shedding tears...

Her eldest son, Wu Muyan, seeing this, quietly shifted closer and held Wu Jingyu's hand tightly. This child, steady and considerate from his youth, gave her comfort. His mother could not bear to leave the place where she had grown up, but with the realm in chaos and the Qing army pressing, moving south was a necessary choice. Some days earlier his mother had already been planning to send a matchmaker to arrange a marriage for him, but had set it aside for the time being because the Qing troops had come south and Nanjing had fallen. He had followed his mother in managing the family business since he was small and knew how hard it was for her, so he was especially willing to share her burdens and made full preparations for inheriting the household in the future.

Her second son, Wu Muen, was a cheerful youth who loved books. Admiring the Jing'e Academy founded by the Prince of Huai, which admitted not only children of the imperial clan but also outstanding local youths, he had asked his mother to let him go to Raozhou to study. After five years under the guidance of the tutor, Mr. Pan, the teacher had given him a courtesy name, "Chengci." Wu Muen thus developed boundless admiration for the Prince of Huai and praised him to everyone he met. When Zhu Changqing settled in the Wu garden residence this time, he had run about more diligently than anyone, and it was he who had taken on the task of finding the cook that very day.

Being close to Zhu Changqing was something Wu Muen could only have wished for. Before, he had only been able to glimpse him from afar when the Prince of Huai inspected the school; now the man was right before his eyes, and he felt it an incomparable honor. He had heard his grandmother mention that Zhu Changqing, then still Heir of Huai, had once set his heart on his mother and wanted to marry her as Heir's consort. He listened to this old story with great relish and was delighted by it, unlike his elder brother, who always told him not to meddle in matters between the Prince of Huai and their mother.

He didn't care. The Prince of Huai was in his heart a good and worthy prince, and if he had a father like that, he could wake up laughing from his dreams.

Looking at the carriage ahead, then at his mother quietly wiping tears, Wu Muen leaned over too. At sixteen, he did not know how to comfort his mother, and only said, "The Prince of Huai is with you, Mama! Don't cry, Mama!" which earned him a fierce glare from Wu Muyan.

"Mama's fine, you needn't worry. Leaving my hometown today, I don't know if I'll ever come back. When I see it again, what will it look like?" Wu Jingyu straightened up, wiped her tears and said, "The Huai princely palace was destroyed by fire, and even if the Prince longs for home, he cannot return. And whether our Jingdezhen's tomorrow will be the same, who knows?"

When she finished, the carriage suddenly fell quiet, and only the sound of wheels rolling over the bluestone street could be heard, farther and farther from Jingdezhen...

And their journey south was only beginning.
